The twin of the Naked Single, seen from the other side: instead of asking what a
square can take, ask where a digit can go.

## The rule

Pick a unit — one row, one column or one box — and pick a digit that unit is
still missing. Look at every empty square in that unit and count how many of
them could still take the digit. If the answer is exactly one, that square is
the digit's only home in the unit, so it must go there. The square may well have
several other candidates; they are irrelevant, because the unit has to contain
the digit somewhere and there is only one place left.

## A worked example

Box 1 is missing a 7. Of its four empty squares, three sit on rows that already
contain a 7 elsewhere, and the fourth is the only one left. That square becomes
a 7 even if it still carried three or four candidates of its own — the 7 is
"hidden" behind them.

## Why it is worth its own rung

This is the technique that makes a puzzle solvable by eye. Scanning is exactly
this deduction done quickly: run a finger along the rows and columns crossing a
box, cross out every square a digit cannot use, and see whether one square
survives.
